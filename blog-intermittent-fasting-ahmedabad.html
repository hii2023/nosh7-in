Intermittent Fasting Guide for Ahmedabad Professionals

By NOSH7 Kitchen & Nutrition Team · 9 min read

Half of Instagram seems to be doing 16:8 right now, and someone in your office WhatsApp group has probably mentioned skipping breakfast this month. Intermittent fasting, eating only within a fixed daily window and fasting for the rest, has gone from a niche bodybuilding trick to a mainstream weight-loss method in a few years. But does it actually work, especially on an Indian vegetarian diet built around rice, roti and dal, and inside a working day full of meetings, traffic and stress in Ahmedabad? The honest answer is that intermittent fasting can genuinely help, but only when you get the details right, and most people get exactly one detail wrong: what they eat once the fast ends.

What Intermittent Fasting Actually Is

Intermittent fasting, often shortened to IF, is not a diet in the traditional sense. It does not tell you what to eat; it tells you when to eat. The most common version is 16:8, meaning you fast for 16 hours and eat all your meals within an 8-hour window, for example between 12 pm and 8 pm. Other versions include 5:2, where you eat normally five days a week and cut calories sharply on two non-consecutive days, and OMAD, or one meal a day, which is a more extreme version of the same idea.

During the fasting hours, your body gradually shifts from using the glucose in your bloodstream for energy to tapping into stored fat and glycogen. This shift, along with lower insulin levels during the fast, is the biological basis behind most of the claimed benefits: better insulin sensitivity, easier fat loss and, for some people, improved focus later in the fast.

Does It Actually Help With Weight Loss?

Mostly, yes, but not for the mystical reason many posts suggest. Intermittent fasting works for weight loss largely because it naturally compresses your eating into fewer hours, which for most people means fewer total meals and snacks, and therefore fewer total calories across the day. If you used to eat breakfast, a mid-morning snack, lunch, evening chai with something fried, and dinner, cutting that down to two solid meals inside an 8-hour window removes several opportunities to overeat without you having to count a single calorie.

The catch is right there in that sentence: it only works if you do not simply cram the same amount of food, or worse, more food, into the shorter window. Eating a large fried snack to "break the fast" and then a heavy, oily dinner two hours later can easily match or exceed what you were eating on a normal five-meal schedule. Fasting controls when you eat. It is still up to you to control what you eat.

Intermittent fasting is a timer, not a magic switch. It only creates a calorie deficit if the food inside your window is honest. Fry your first meal of the day and the timer stops mattering.

The One Mistake That Undoes Everything

After talking to gym-goers and office professionals across Satellite, Bodakdev and Prahlad Nagar who have tried intermittent fasting, one pattern shows up again and again: they break a 16-hour fast with a plate of fried snacks, a couple of sweets, or a large, oily thali, because "I have earned it." The fast created willpower and discipline for 16 hours, and then the first bite of food undid the entire nutritional point of the exercise. A fast followed by fried food is still fried food; the fasting hours before it do not cancel out the oil, refined carbs and empty calories that follow.

The second common mistake is protein. With only two meals to fit everything into, an Indian vegetarian diet needs deliberate planning to hit even 60 to 70 grams of protein a day. Skip that planning and you end up losing muscle along with fat, which shows up as looking "thinner but softer" rather than genuinely leaner. How to Do Intermittent Fasting Right

If you want to try 16:8, these five steps separate the people who see real results from the people who quit after two weeks feeling worse than before.

  1. Start with 12:12, then extend gradually. Jumping straight to a 16-hour fast is unnecessary and often triggers overeating. Extend your fasting window by an hour every few days until you comfortably reach 16 hours.
  2. Break your fast with protein and fibre, not fried food. A bowl of sprouts, a high-protein salad or dal with vegetables restarts digestion gently and keeps you full, unlike a sugar or oil spike that leaves you hungry again in an hour.
  3. Hit your protein target inside the window. Since you have fewer meals, build each one around 25 to 35 grams of protein from dals, paneer, curd, tofu, sprouts and seeds so you do not fall short across the day.
  4. Stay hydrated through the fasting hours. Water, black coffee and plain tea do not break a fast and genuinely reduce hunger pangs. Dehydration is often mistaken for hunger.
  5. Judge it by energy and consistency, not the scale alone. If you feel more focused, less bloated and can sustain the schedule for months, it is working. If you feel constantly irritable or your workouts suffer, the schedule needs adjusting or is not right for you.

Fasting Pairs Well With a Simple Eating Window

The best-kept secret of intermittent fasting is that it actually makes healthy eating easier, not harder, because you only have to plan two good meals instead of five. That is precisely where a lot of people stumble: two meals need to be more deliberate than five smaller ones, since there is no third or fourth chance to fix a nutrient gap later in the day. Frequently Asked Questions

Does intermittent fasting actually help with weight loss?

Intermittent fasting can help with weight loss, but mostly because it naturally limits the hours in which you eat, which often lowers total calorie intake. If you eat the same amount of food, especially fried or sugary food, inside a shorter window, you will not lose weight. The fasting schedule is a tool for calorie control, not a metabolic shortcut that works regardless of what you eat.

Is 16:8 intermittent fasting safe for Indian vegetarians?

Yes, for most healthy adults, 16:8 is generally safe, but vegetarians need to plan more carefully because plant protein is less concentrated than meat or eggs. With a shorter eating window, it becomes harder to fit in enough protein, fibre, iron and vitamin B12 across just two meals, so every meal inside the window needs to be dense in nutrients rather than just filling.

What is the biggest mistake people make with intermittent fasting?

The most common mistake is breaking the fast with fried snacks, sweets or a heavy, oily thali, then eating the same way for the second meal too. This gives you all the discipline of fasting with none of the nutrition benefit, and often more sugar and fried food overall because people feel they have earned it after fasting. What you eat inside the window matters more than the fasting itself.

Who should avoid intermittent fasting?

Pregnant or breastfeeding women, people with a history of disordered eating, people with diabetes on insulin or other blood-sugar-lowering medication, and anyone underweight or managing a chronic illness should not start intermittent fasting without first talking to a doctor. Skipping meals can affect blood sugar and hormones differently for these groups, so medical guidance comes first.
